## Q1 — Survey ("You're alienating people. They're saying no." vs. the 2026 survey)

> It's great that we survey our patrons, but as with all surveys, the challenge is finding a representative sample. Even with great diligence, sampling bias is hard to avoid. In our surveys, those who encounter and respond are nearly all active library users. Those who have given up on the library were not likely to encounter or take the survey. There are many things to love about the library, and I'm pleased that there are many active and appreciative patrons. I also hear from people who want to use the resources, but are unwilling to accept the activism in material on shelves and promotional layouts and Celebrations displays. Having given up on the library, they are unlikely to see a survey or take the time to fill it out.

---

## Q2 — Displays ("rainbow and activist"; neutral principle; who decides "activist")

> With the First Amendment being foundational to our library organization (and nation), I'd push back against the idea that a patriotic display would be activist or divisive. A library should be fully comfortable with patriotic displays. However, I wouldn't want to see the library take a stand in support or opposition to any religion. Nor should it take the side of any political party or cause. I think it's clear to any fair-minded observer that there are fault lines in our society, and if the library puts up displays that push strongly in one direction on divisive issues, we will alienate half of our population and potential patrons. I don't think it's difficult to determine hot-button topics and decide that on those issues, the library will let people come into the library without being greeted by displays that clearly indicate the library has decided what is important for its patrons to see, accept and believe on those topics.

---

## Q3 — Picture book / Sund / "gender orientation" classification

> The American Library Assoction's Library Bill of Rights website states: "We affirm the responsibility and the right of all parents and guardians to guide their own children's use of the library and its resources and services."

> There are many parents who want access to books for discussion with their children about sex and sexuality or that focus on sexual information, including human growth and development, books encouraging children to consider sexual identity (of any orientation), and books about gender fluidity. And there are many parents who don't want their young children to stumble across those books while browsing in the children's section. The solution is to put these books on a higher shelf, accessible to parents or children who seek them out. My bullet points – which were not direction, but were points to discuss – suggested that we could make our library more open and welcoming to more parents and families by moving these books. This is not the scenario in Sund v. City of Wichita Falls, which mandated that movement based on community petition. I'm suggesting that our own staff look at our children's section in a different way. They are well-trained. It wouldn't be hard to tell which books are of concern. It would be better, in my opinion, that we no longer require that all parents who visit the library accept the fact that their children will likely come upon books about human sexuality while in the youngest children's section. Many parents in our five counties will say no to the library entirely rather than agree to that situation that TRL is putting them in. I don't think it has to be that way.

## Q5 — Bring to board as formal policy? Directed staff to act?

> No and no. I still believe that there are common-sense solutions to be had on this – small modifications of library practice within current policies that would not require anyone to violate their beliefs, would not remove any books from our libraries, would censor nothing, would still allow parents to check out any books they'd like for their kids – or kids to check them out, for that matter – but would move books on sex, sexuality and gender fluidity out of areas where kids would stumble across them without parents first being aware and having the opportunity to review and talk with their kids about them.

> Let's orient TRL toward supporting parents in the way recommended by the American Library Association's Library Bill of Rights website: "We affirm the responsibility and the right of all parents and guardians to guide their own children's use of the library and its resources and services."

> There are many parents who are eager to explore and use the library resources they pay for, but only when they have a way to do that which allows them to protect their children's innocence.

---

## Q6 — "No dividing kids from parents, no suggesting they hide things from them" / RCW 42.56.310

> This refers to a pamphlet that has been on display in the children's area for several years that states "No matter your age, you have the right to keep your library account private. Check with library staff anytime with questions about your account." Many parents have reasonably interpreted this as assuring children that they can hide things from their parents. This pamphlet has been controversial. In my recent meeting with our interim executive director, she informed me that this language has been removed from the pamphlet. That's a positive move, and it shows me that common-sense changes are possible within the current framework of policies and pro-Freedom to Read philosophies.

## Q7 — Why pursue board-unenactable aims during the levy campaign?

> I brought these points into a conversation with library leadership with the hope of kicking off a conversation on ways to bring people back into TRL who have abandoned a library system that they see as having abandoned them. I can't ask people to increase their taxes when they think the library system is actively opposed to their values and that it isn't a place they will ever bring their children. I think we can change that while still keeping our library's a vibrant place of wide-ranging information – without removing any books, but through recognizing the value of creating a children's section that parents can bring their children to without worry, and by collecting books together that parents are free to browse, check out, and share with their children if they'd like.

## Follow-up Q1 — Harrington (lunch; "censorship"; "opposes every item")

> We did have a conversation over lunch that covered many items, from the budget situation to leadership to sharing facts and ideas from different discussions we'd had with various people and community groups. It was a good conversation. It ended with me bringing up these general concerns. It's accurate to say that we did not see eye to eye on these points..

> I don't see this as censorship at all. Nothing is removed from the library. Parents have full rights to find and share anything they'd like with their children. Children have full rights to check out these materials and browse them – but will also be protected from stumbling across materials that in the view of many parents are inappropriate for them. This is a deeply personal issue that hits at the heart of identity, morality and personal development. Let's make these resources available but not force them on people.

> As far as opposing "every item," if that's what she said and meant, I'll note that before I even brought these ideas forward, library leadership had already decided to address the issues behind one of them, removing language from a children's flyer that seemed to suggest kids hide things from their parents. So these items apparently are not completely objectionable.

## Follow-up — "In your own words, what was the problem you set out to solve?"

> This began with my desire to be straightforward and open, and to work with the leaders of this organization to find solutions. One nagging problem is that many families in my community and across our district are not using the library because they have come to see it as actively advocating against their strongly held beliefs.

> For instance, a Packwood parent said, "I cannot trust that the library is not putting inappropriate material out in full display for them to see and access, simply by walking into the building or into the kids room. I should not have to feel that way!"

> These folks aren't being heard and I'm trying to give them a voice.

> Parents should have the right to a safe children's section. They should have one small walled garden where their children can begin to explore the library and the world of ideas with their innocence protected.

> I also proposed a change to the library's mandated "Celebrations." Instead of one-size-fits-all diversity, I suggested that individual libraries be given a choice on what to celebrate each month. Many people in the Olympia community might love having a Pride display in June, for instance. Other smaller communities might better appreciate a different celebration. It's not the place of TRL to dictate. Let's let the true diversity of our district shine, rather than mandating one approved flavor of diversity from headquarters.

## Follow-up — "be beige, not rainbow" attribution

> That wasn't my phrase. Some time ago, a community leader used that phrase in a discussion about displays and celebrations in the library. This individual said that our library should be a little less rainbow colored, and a little more politically beige. I included that in my discussion points with our interim executive director.

> That phrase bothered some people. They love the vibrancy of the library, and I do, too. I'm not trying to get rid of all the colors. A beautiful work of art isn't beige, but the canvas is. That might be a helpful way to think about it. The resources and information in the library provide a rich palette with a million different colors. The library shouldn't paint the rainbow. It should let patrons sample its riches and create their own works of art, with support for parents who want to guide their children along that journey.
